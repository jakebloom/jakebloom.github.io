## Dvar Torah for Parashat Vayehi

_On December 14th 2025, two terrorists killed fifteen people at a Chanukah celebration at Bondi Beach, where I grew up. This is a copy of the Dvar Torah I gave at Congregation Beth Sholom in San Francisco, on January 3rd 2026_

In this week’s parsha, we complete Joseph’s story of resilience. He has gone from servant to prisoner, and is now viceroy of Egypt, at the peak of his power, when his father dies. The whole family makes the trek back to Canaan to bury Jacob. They have all experienced loss, they are all grieving, and they are doing a physically and emotionally difficult return journey to Egypt.

There’s a midrash that tells us that on the way, they pass the pit that the brothers threw Joseph into, all those years ago. They stop at the pit, Joseph looks into it, and he blesses the pit, he says “Blessed be the place where HaShem performed a miracle for me”.

The brothers are confused and they ask Joseph for forgiveness for the incident. Joseph says:

וְאַתֶּ֕ם חֲשַׁבְתֶּ֥ם עָלַ֖י רָעָ֑ה אֱלֹהִים֙ חֲשָׁבָ֣הּ לְטֹבָ֔ה

“Although you intended me harm, G-d intended it for good”.

Looking back at Joseph’s life, he was a master of turning adversity into resilience, opportunity and success. He was thrown in prison, so he became the assistant warden. He parlayed that into interpreting dreams, and then advising the Pharaoh on agricultural matters. He took the tragedies he experienced and **rededicated** them into something he was proud of.

For those of you I have not met, my name is Jake Bloom and I grew up in Bondi Beach in Sydney. I am one of only forty thousand Jews from Sydney, and on the first night of Chanukah, 15 of us were killed, dozens injured, two are still in comas fighting for their lives.

Bondi is the centre of the Jewish Community in Sydney. It’s our Upper West Side, our Squirrel Hill, our Golders Green and Hampstead Heath. Growing up, we called the North End of the beach “Jew Hill”, because that’s where we hung out together on sunny days. And pretty much every day was sunny.

I’ve just returned from two weeks spent with my family and friends in Sydney. It was the kind of trip that had a lot of long hugs, deep silences and conversations that went like this: 

“Hey mate, how are you doing? I’m doing okay, and you? Yeah mate, I’m doing okay”. And that’s actually very emotional for Aussies.

The small size of the community means that we are all affected. Everyone knows someone who was there. Close friends of mine have been rolled onto extra shifts working as volunteer security or paramedics as we try to heal and move on.

Now, I apologise for talking about Chanukah in January. But on Chanukah we celebrate the miracle of the **rededication** of the temple. It’s the literal translation of the word Chanukah. The Maccabees rededicated the Beit HaMikdash from a place where a tragedy occurred to a place where a miracle occurred. Joseph rededicated the pit from a place where he was estranged from his family, to a place where he was united with them. 

In the past three weeks, I personally witnessed the Jewish community in Sydney performing the Chanukah miracle.

After the attack, the message from the leaders of the community was clear - we will not cower. On the final night of Chanukah, we hosted a memorial, back at Bondi Beach, where twenty thousand people attended. That’s half the Jews living in Sydney. We were joined by thousands of allies showing their support.

Three weeks ago, Bondi Beach was the site of the worst terror attack in Australian history. Now, Bondi Beach is the site of the largest Jewish gathering in Australian history.

At the memorial, David Ossip, the president of the local equivalent of the Jewish Federation said, and I quote: “The answer cannot be to hide our Judaism, and the answer cannot be to just add more security and build bigger compounds”. Instead, we committed to return and hold a bigger and more joyful Chanukah celebration next year. Chabad set up a tent out the back of the Bondi Pavilion where thousands of community members came to give tzedakah and wrap tefillin.


The wider Australian community helped us in our rededication. The lifeguards hosted a paddle out at the beach which thousands of surfers attended. People from all corners of the country donated millions of dollars, not only to the victims and the heroes, but also to the local businesses who have seen their traffic drop.

Last week, I drove down to Bondi by myself and went for a run along the promenade. I happened to come across some of the guys I went to high school with. It turns out, they were there for a Jewish Run Club that had formed that day. I joined them, and about two hundred of us ran up and down the beach, past the park and the bridge where the attack took place.

We rededicated our neighbourhood to be a space where we belong not just in safety, but as a place where we can thrive as a Jewish Community. 

My friends, I wish I could tell you that my friends and family back home are better than ever. They will be, but there is still a lot of healing and a lot of work to do. I wish I could tell you that we have witnessed the last antisemitic terror attack. I wish I could tell you that we don’t need more security and bigger compounds. 

I can’t tell you those things today. But I can encourage you to take tragedy and hardship, whether personal, professional or communal, and follow the example of Joseph, follow the example of our brothers and sisters in Sydney, perform the miracle of Chanukah and rededicate it to positive change, to resilience, and to the future.

Thank you, Shabbat Shalom.
